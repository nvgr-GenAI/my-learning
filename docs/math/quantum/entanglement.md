## Introduction

Entanglement is the defining feature of quantum mechanics that distinguishes it from classical physics. When quantum systems become entangled, their states become correlated in ways that cannot be explained by any classical probability distribution. This "spooky action at a distance" (Einstein's phrase) is not a bug but a feature—one that powers quantum computing, quantum communication, and potentially quantum machine learning.

## 8.4.1 Separable and Entangled States

**Definition 8.4.1 (Separable State)**
A pure state $|\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$ of a bipartite system is **separable** if it can be written as a tensor product:

$$|\psi\rangle = |\phi\rangle_A \otimes |\chi\rangle_B$$

for some states $|\phi\rangle_A \in \mathcal{H}_A$ and $|\chi\rangle_B \in \mathcal{H}_B$.

**Definition 8.4.2 (Entangled State)**
A pure state $|\psi\rangle$ is **entangled** if it is not separable. That is, there do not exist states $|\phi\rangle_A$ and $|\chi\rangle_B$ such that:

$$|\psi\rangle = |\phi\rangle_A \otimes |\chi\rangle_B$$

### Examples

**Example 8.4.1 (Separable State)**
Consider two qubits:

$$|\psi\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle) \otimes |0\rangle = |00\rangle + |10\rangle$$

This can be factored as $|\psi\rangle = |\phi\rangle_A \otimes |\chi\rangle_B$ where $|\phi\rangle_A = |0\rangle + |1\rangle$ (unnormalized) and $|\chi\rangle_B = |0\rangle$. Therefore, it is separable.

**Example 8.4.2 (Simplest Separable State)**
The computational basis state $|00\rangle$ is separable. Explicitly, using column vectors:

$$|0\rangle_A \otimes |0\rangle_B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \otimes \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = |00\rangle$$

The tensor product factors cleanly, so the state is separable. Similarly, $|01\rangle$, $|10\rangle$, and $|11\rangle$ are all separable.

**Example 8.4.3 (Entangled State -- Factoring Attempt Fails)**
The state:

$$|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$$

is entangled. To see this, suppose it could be written as:

$$|\Phi^+\rangle = (\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle) \otimes (\gamma|0\rangle + \delta|1\rangle)$$

Expanding the right side:

$$= \alpha\gamma|00\rangle + \alpha\delta|01\rangle + \beta\gamma|10\rangle + \beta\delta|11\rangle$$

For this to equal $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$, we need:
- $\alpha\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$
- $\alpha\delta = 0$
- $\beta\gamma = 0$
- $\beta\delta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$

From the second equation, either $\alpha = 0$ or $\delta = 0$. If $\alpha = 0$, the first equation gives $0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, a contradiction. If $\delta = 0$, the fourth equation gives a contradiction. Therefore, no such factorization exists.

### Visual Representation

```
Separable State:              Entangled State:
┌─────────┐                   ┌─────────────────┐
│ Qubit A │ ──independent──   │  Qubit A ←──┐   │
└─────────┘                   │             │   │
                              │  correlated │   │
┌─────────┐                   │             │   │
│ Qubit B │ ──independent──   │  Qubit B ←──┘   │
└─────────┘                   └─────────────────┘

Measurement of A:             Measurement of A:
No effect on B                Instantly affects B's state
```

## 8.4.2 Bell States

The four **Bell states** (or **EPR pairs**) form a maximally entangled orthonormal basis for two qubits.

**Definition 8.4.3 (Bell States)**

$$\begin{align}
|\Phi^+\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle) \\
|\Phi^-\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle - |11\rangle) \\
|\Psi^+\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|01\rangle + |10\rangle) \\
|\Psi^-\rangle &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|01\rangle - |10\rangle)
\end{align}$$

### Derivation from Pauli Matrices

The Bell states can be generated by applying the **Bell circuit** to $|00\rangle$:

```
|0⟩ ──H──●──
         │
|0⟩ ─────X──
```

Starting with $|00\rangle$:
1. Hadamard on first qubit: $H|0\rangle \otimes |0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle) \otimes |0\rangle$
2. CNOT: $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle) = |\Phi^+\rangle$

The other Bell states are obtained by applying Pauli operators:

$$\begin{align}
|\Phi^+\rangle &= (I \otimes I)|\Phi^+\rangle \\
|\Psi^+\rangle &= (X \otimes I)|\Phi^+\rangle \\
|\Phi^-\rangle &= (Z \otimes I)|\Phi^+\rangle \\
|\Psi^-\rangle &= (ZX \otimes I)|\Phi^+\rangle
\end{align}$$

### Properties of Bell States

**Theorem 8.4.1 (Maximally Entangled)**
The Bell states are maximally entangled: each subsystem, when measured individually, yields a completely random outcome (50/50 for 0 and 1).

**Proof:** Consider $|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$. The reduced density matrix for qubit A is:

$$\rho_A = \text{Tr}_B(|\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+|) = \frac{1}{2}|0\rangle\langle 0| + \frac{1}{2}|1\rangle\langle 1| = \frac{I}{2}$$

This is the maximally mixed state, indicating complete randomness. $\square$

**Example 8.4.4 (Partial Trace of a Bell State)**
Let us compute $\rho_A = \text{Tr}_B(|\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+|)$ step by step. First, write the density matrix:

$$|\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+| = \frac{1}{2}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)(\langle 00| + \langle 11|) = \frac{1}{2}(|00\rangle\langle 00| + |00\rangle\langle 11| + |11\rangle\langle 00| + |11\rangle\langle 11|)$$

Now trace over subsystem $B$ by summing $\langle j|_B (\cdot) |j\rangle_B$ for $j = 0, 1$:

$$\text{Tr}_B = \langle 0|_B(\cdot)|0\rangle_B + \langle 1|_B(\cdot)|1\rangle_B$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}\big(|0\rangle\langle 0| \underbrace{\langle 0|0\rangle}_{1}\underbrace{\langle 0|0\rangle}_{1} + |0\rangle\langle 1| \underbrace{\langle 0|1\rangle}_{0}\underbrace{\langle 0|0\rangle}_{1} + |1\rangle\langle 0| \underbrace{\langle 0|0\rangle}_{1}\underbrace{\langle 1|0\rangle}_{0} + |1\rangle\langle 1| \underbrace{\langle 0|1\rangle}_{0}\underbrace{\langle 1|0\rangle}_{0}\big)$$

$$+ \frac{1}{2}\big(|0\rangle\langle 0| \cdot 0 + |0\rangle\langle 1| \cdot 0 + |1\rangle\langle 0| \cdot 0 + |1\rangle\langle 1| \underbrace{\langle 1|1\rangle}_{1}\underbrace{\langle 1|1\rangle}_{1}\big)$$

$$\rho_A = \frac{1}{2}|0\rangle\langle 0| + \frac{1}{2}|1\rangle\langle 1| = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{I}{2}$$

This confirms $\rho_A$ is the maximally mixed state: each measurement outcome is equally likely.

**Theorem 8.4.2 (Perfect Correlation)**
When both qubits of $|\Phi^+\rangle$ are measured in the computational basis, the outcomes are always identical.

**Proof:** The state $|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$ has support only on $|00\rangle$ and $|11\rangle$. Measuring yields:
- $|00\rangle$ with probability $|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|^2 = \frac{1}{2}$
- $|11\rangle$ with probability $|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|^2 = \frac{1}{2}$

In both cases, the measurement outcomes are identical. $\square$

> **Quantum Connection:** Bell states are the fundamental resource for quantum communication protocols. Every quantum teleportation, superdense coding, and entanglement-based quantum key distribution protocol begins with a shared Bell pair.

## 8.4.3 The EPR Paradox

In 1935, Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen (EPR) published a thought experiment arguing that quantum mechanics was incomplete.

### The EPR Argument

**Setup:** Prepare two particles in the state $|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$ and separate them by a large distance.

**Observation:** When Alice measures her particle in the computational basis:
- If she gets 0, Bob's state instantly "collapses" to $|0\rangle$
- If she gets 1, Bob's state instantly "collapses" to $|1\rangle$

**EPR's Reasoning:**
1. The measurement outcome is perfectly correlated.
2. No signal can travel faster than light (locality).
3. Therefore, Bob's particle must have "known" its state even before Alice measured.
4. Conclusion: Quantum mechanics is incomplete; there exist "hidden variables" that determine the outcome in advance.

### Einstein's "Spooky Action at a Distance"

Einstein objected to the idea that measuring one particle could instantaneously affect a distant particle. He believed in **local realism**:

- **Realism:** Physical properties exist independent of measurement.
- **Locality:** Measurements at one location cannot instantaneously affect distant locations.

**Definition 8.4.4 (Local Hidden Variable Theory)**
A **local hidden variable (LHV) theory** posits that:
1. Each particle carries hidden information $\lambda$ that predetermines all measurement outcomes.
2. The measurement outcome at location A depends only on the local measurement setting and $\lambda$.
3. No instantaneous action at a distance occurs.

```
Classical (LHV) Model:
┌──────────┐
│ Source   │  creates particles with hidden variable λ
└────┬─────┘
     │
     ├────→ Alice's particle (carries λ)
     │
     └────→ Bob's particle (carries λ)

Alice measures → outcome A(a, λ)
Bob measures   → outcome B(b, λ)

Outcomes determined by λ, not by each other's measurements
```

## 8.4.4 Bell's Theorem and CHSH Inequality

In 1964, John Bell proved that **no local hidden variable theory can reproduce all predictions of quantum mechanics**.

### Bell's Theorem Statement

**Theorem 8.4.3 (Bell's Theorem)**
There exist quantum states and measurements such that the correlations between measurement outcomes violate any inequality satisfied by all local hidden variable theories.

### The CHSH Inequality

The **Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt (CHSH)** inequality provides a concrete test.

**Setup:** Alice and Bob each choose from two measurement settings:
- Alice: measures $A_0$ or $A_1$ (each with outcomes $\pm 1$)
- Bob: measures $B_0$ or $B_1$ (each with outcomes $\pm 1$)

Define the **CHSH correlation**:

$$S = E(A_0 B_0) + E(A_0 B_1) + E(A_1 B_0) - E(A_1 B_1)$$

where $E(A_i B_j) = \langle A_i B_j \rangle$ is the expectation value of the product of outcomes.

**Theorem 8.4.4 (CHSH Inequality)**
For any local hidden variable theory:

$$|S| \leq 2$$

**Proof Sketch:** For any value of $\lambda$, the outcomes are deterministic: $A_i(\lambda), B_j(\lambda) \in \{-1, +1\}$. Then:

$$\begin{align}
S(\lambda) &= A_0 B_0 + A_0 B_1 + A_1 B_0 - A_1 B_1 \\
&= A_0(B_0 + B_1) + A_1(B_0 - B_1)
\end{align}$$

Since $B_0, B_1 \in \{-1, +1\}$, either $B_0 = B_1$ or $B_0 = -B_1$:
- If $B_0 = B_1$: $S(\lambda) = A_0 \cdot 2B_0 = \pm 2$
- If $B_0 = -B_1$: $S(\lambda) = A_1 \cdot 2B_0 = \pm 2$

Thus $|S(\lambda)| \leq 2$ for all $\lambda$, and averaging over $\lambda$ preserves this bound. $\square$

### Quantum Violation: Tsirelson's Bound

**Theorem 8.4.5 (Tsirelson's Bound)**
Quantum mechanics allows:

$$|S| \leq 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.828$$

**Example 8.4.5 (Achieving $2\sqrt{2}$)**
Consider the state $|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$ and measurements:
- Alice: $A_0 = Z$, $A_1 = X$
- Bob: $B_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(Z + X)$, $B_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(Z - X)$

Computing the expectation values:

$$E(A_0 B_0) = E(A_0 B_1) = E(A_1 B_0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \quad E(A_1 B_1) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$$

Therefore:

$$S = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} - \left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = \frac{4}{\sqrt{2}} = 2\sqrt{2}$$

This violates the classical bound of 2, confirming that quantum mechanics cannot be explained by local hidden variables.

**Example 8.4.6 (CHSH: Computing One Expectation Value Step by Step)**
Let us verify $E(A_0 B_0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ from the previous example. We need $\langle \Phi^+ | (Z \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(Z + X)) | \Phi^+ \rangle$.

First, compute $(Z \otimes Z)|\Phi^+\rangle$:

$$Z \otimes Z \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}((+1)(+1)|00\rangle + (-1)(-1)|11\rangle) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle) = |\Phi^+\rangle$$

So $\langle\Phi^+|Z \otimes Z|\Phi^+\rangle = 1$. Next, compute $(Z \otimes X)|\Phi^+\rangle$:

$$Z \otimes X \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|01\rangle + (-1)|10\rangle) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|01\rangle - |10\rangle)$$

So $\langle\Phi^+|Z \otimes X|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{2}(\langle 00| + \langle 11|)(|01\rangle - |10\rangle) = 0$. Combining:

$$E(A_0 B_0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot 1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot 0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707 \; \checkmark$$

```
CHSH Inequality Visualization:

Classical Bound:     |S| ≤ 2
                     ├─────────┤
                    -2         2

Quantum Bound:       |S| ≤ 2√2
                     ├─────────────┤
                  -2√2           2√2

Quantum achieves ~2.828, exceeding classical limit!
```

> **Quantum Connection:** Bell inequality violations are the foundation of **device-independent quantum cryptography**, where security is guaranteed by the laws of physics rather than computational assumptions.

## 8.4.5 No-Communication Theorem

While entanglement creates non-local correlations, it cannot be used to transmit information faster than light.

**Theorem 8.4.6 (No-Communication Theorem)**
Measurement on one part of an entangled system cannot be used to signal information to a distant observer examining only the other part.

**Proof:** Consider a bipartite state $\rho_{AB}$. The reduced density matrix for Bob's system is:

$$\rho_B = \text{Tr}_A(\rho_{AB})$$

When Alice performs a measurement described by POVM elements $\{M_a\}$, the post-measurement state (conditioned on outcome $a$) is:

$$\rho_{AB}^a = \frac{(M_a \otimes I) \rho_{AB} (M_a^\dagger \otimes I)}{\text{Tr}[(M_a \otimes I) \rho_{AB} (M_a^\dagger \otimes I)]}$$

Bob's reduced state, averaged over Alice's outcomes:

$$\begin{align}
\sum_a p(a) \rho_B^a &= \sum_a \text{Tr}_A\left[(M_a \otimes I) \rho_{AB} (M_a^\dagger \otimes I)\right] \\
&= \text{Tr}_A\left[\left(\sum_a M_a M_a^\dagger\right) \otimes I \, \rho_{AB}\right] \\
&= \text{Tr}_A[\rho_{AB}] = \rho_B
\end{align}$$

The last step uses $\sum_a M_a M_a^\dagger = I$ (completeness). Thus, Bob's density matrix is unchanged in expectation, regardless of what Alice measures. $\square$

**Interpretation:** While individual measurement results are correlated, Bob cannot determine from his local measurements alone whether Alice has measured her system or what outcome she obtained.

```
No-Communication Principle:

Alice ────[Measure]──→ Outcome "a"
  │
  │ Entangled pair
  │
Bob ──────[Observe]──→ Statistics unchanged

Bob's local statistics: ρ_B = Tr_A(ρ_AB)
Independent of Alice's measurement choice!

To learn Alice's outcome, Bob needs classical communication.
```

## 8.4.6 Entanglement as a Resource

Entanglement enables protocols impossible with classical resources.

### Quantum Teleportation

**Protocol 8.4.1 (Quantum Teleportation)**
Alice wishes to send an unknown quantum state $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ to Bob using:
- A shared entangled Bell pair $|\Phi^+\rangle_{AB}$
- 2 classical bits of communication

**Steps:**
1. **Initial state:** $|\psi\rangle_0 \otimes |\Phi^+\rangle_{AB} = (\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle)_0 \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)_{AB}$

2. **Alice's Bell measurement:** Alice performs a Bell basis measurement on qubits 0 and A, obtaining one of four outcomes (00, 01, 10, 11).

3. **Bob's correction:** Based on Alice's classical message, Bob applies a Pauli correction:
   - 00 → $I$ (do nothing)
   - 01 → $X$ (bit flip)
   - 10 → $Z$ (phase flip)
   - 11 → $ZX$ (both)

4. **Result:** Bob's qubit is now in state $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$.

**Key Properties:**
- The original qubit 0 is destroyed (no-cloning theorem).
- 2 classical bits must be communicated (no faster-than-light signaling).
- The protocol works for any unknown $|\psi\rangle$.

```
Quantum Teleportation Circuit:

|ψ⟩ ──●──H──M──→ outcome (i,j) → send 2 classical bits
      │     │
|0⟩ ──H─●───X──M──→ outcome (i,j)
        │
|0⟩ ────X──────────X^j──Z^i──→ |ψ⟩ reconstructed!

Entangled pair shared beforehand
```

> **Quantum Connection:** Quantum teleportation is fundamental to quantum networks and distributed quantum computing. It enables quantum communication without physically transporting quantum systems.

### Superdense Coding

**Protocol 8.4.2 (Superdense Coding)**
Alice sends 2 classical bits to Bob using only 1 qubit, assuming they share a Bell pair.

**Steps:**
1. **Shared resource:** $|\Phi^+\rangle_{AB} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$

2. **Alice's encoding:** To send bits $(b_1, b_2)$, Alice applies:
   - $(0,0)$ → $I$ (resulting state: $|\Phi^+\rangle$)
   - $(0,1)$ → $X$ (resulting state: $|\Psi^+\rangle$)
   - $(1,0)$ → $Z$ (resulting state: $|\Phi^-\rangle$)
   - $(1,1)$ → $ZX$ (resulting state: $|\Psi^-\rangle$)

3. **Alice sends her qubit to Bob:** Bob now has both qubits.

4. **Bob's Bell measurement:** Bob measures in the Bell basis, perfectly distinguishing the four states, recovering $(b_1, b_2)$.

**Key Properties:**
- 2 classical bits transmitted using 1 qubit.
- Requires pre-shared entanglement.
- Achieves the channel capacity of a noiseless qubit channel.

## 8.4.7 Schmidt Decomposition

The **Schmidt decomposition** quantifies entanglement by revealing the minimal basis needed to express a bipartite state.

**Theorem 8.4.7 (Schmidt Decomposition)**
For any pure state $|\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$, there exist orthonormal bases $\{|i_A\rangle\}$ for $\mathcal{H}_A$ and $\{|i_B\rangle\}$ for $\mathcal{H}_B$ such that:

$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^r \lambda_i |i_A\rangle \otimes |i_B\rangle$$

where $\lambda_i > 0$ are the **Schmidt coefficients** satisfying $\sum_i \lambda_i^2 = 1$, and $r$ is the **Schmidt rank**.

**Proof Sketch:**
1. Write $|\psi\rangle = \sum_{i,j} c_{ij} |i\rangle_A \otimes |j\rangle_B$ in some orthonormal bases.
2. Form the matrix $C = [c_{ij}]$ and compute its singular value decomposition: $C = U \Sigma V^\dagger$.
3. The singular values $\sigma_i$ become the Schmidt coefficients $\lambda_i = \sigma_i$.
4. The columns of $U$ and $V$ define the Schmidt bases $\{|i_A\rangle\}$ and $\{|i_B\rangle\}$. $\square$

**Definition 8.4.5 (Schmidt Rank)**
The **Schmidt rank** $r$ is the number of non-zero Schmidt coefficients. A state is:
- **Separable** if and only if $r = 1$.
- **Entangled** if and only if $r \geq 2$.
- **Maximally entangled** (for equal dimensions) if $\lambda_i = 1/\sqrt{d}$ for all $i$.

**Example 8.4.7 (Schmidt Decomposition of Bell State)**
For $|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$:

$$|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle_A \otimes |0\rangle_B + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle_A \otimes |1\rangle_B$$

This is already in Schmidt form with $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $r = 2$ (maximally entangled).

**Example 8.4.8 (Schmidt Decomposition via SVD)**
Consider the state $|\psi\rangle = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}|00\rangle + \frac{1}{2}|11\rangle$. Write the coefficient matrix:

$$C = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}$$

This matrix is already diagonal, so the SVD is trivial: $C = I \cdot C \cdot I$. The singular values are $\sigma_1 = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\sigma_2 = \frac{1}{2}$. The Schmidt decomposition is:

$$|\psi\rangle = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}|0\rangle_A|0\rangle_B + \frac{1}{2}|1\rangle_A|1\rangle_B$$

with Schmidt coefficients $\lambda_1 = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.866$, $\lambda_2 = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5$, and Schmidt rank $r = 2$. Since $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$, this is entangled but **not** maximally entangled. Verification: $\lambda_1^2 + \lambda_2^2 = \frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = 1$ $\checkmark$.

**Example 8.4.9 (Schmidt Decomposition of a Separable State)**
Consider:

$$|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{2}|00\rangle + \frac{1}{2}|01\rangle + \frac{1}{2}|10\rangle + \frac{1}{2}|11\rangle$$

Write as matrix:

$$C = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

The SVD gives singular values $\sigma_1 = 1, \sigma_2 = 0$, so:

$$|\psi\rangle = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)_A \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)_B$$

Schmidt rank $r = 1$, hence separable.

### Relationship to Reduced Density Matrices

**Theorem 8.4.8**
The Schmidt coefficients $\{\lambda_i\}$ are the square roots of the non-zero eigenvalues of the reduced density matrices:

$$\rho_A = \text{Tr}_B(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|) = \sum_i \lambda_i^2 |i_A\rangle\langle i_A|$$

$$\rho_B = \text{Tr}_A(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|) = \sum_i \lambda_i^2 |i_B\rangle\langle i_B|$$

Thus, $\rho_A$ and $\rho_B$ share the same non-zero eigenvalues.

## 8.4.8 Measures of Entanglement

Quantifying entanglement is essential for understanding its role as a resource.

### Entanglement Entropy

**Definition 8.4.6 (Entanglement Entropy)**
For a pure bipartite state $|\psi\rangle_{AB}$, the **entanglement entropy** is the von Neumann entropy of the reduced density matrix:

$$S_E = -\text{Tr}(\rho_A \log_2 \rho_A) = -\sum_i \lambda_i^2 \log_2 \lambda_i^2$$

where $\lambda_i$ are the Schmidt coefficients.

**Properties:**
- $S_E = 0$ if and only if the state is separable.
- $S_E = \log_2 d$ for a maximally entangled state in dimension $d \times d$.
- $S_E$ quantifies the amount of classical communication needed to create the state using LOCC (local operations and classical communication).

**Example 8.4.10 (Entanglement Entropy of Bell State)**
For the Bell state $|\Phi^+\rangle$ with $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$:

$$S_E = -2 \left(\frac{1}{2} \log_2 \frac{1}{2}\right) = -2 \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = 1 \text{ ebit}$$

One **ebit** (entanglement bit) is the entanglement of a maximally entangled qubit pair.

**Example 8.4.11 (Entanglement Entropy of a Partially Entangled State)**
Consider $|\psi\rangle = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}|00\rangle + \frac{1}{2}|11\rangle$ from Example 8.4.8, with Schmidt coefficients $\lambda_1 = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, $\lambda_2 = \frac{1}{2}$:

$$S_E = -\left(\frac{3}{4}\log_2 \frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4}\log_2 \frac{1}{4}\right)$$

$$= -\left(\frac{3}{4}(\log_2 3 - 2) + \frac{1}{4}(-2)\right) = -\frac{3}{4}\log_2 3 + \frac{3}{2} + \frac{1}{2}$$

$$= 2 - \frac{3}{4}\log_2 3 \approx 2 - 1.189 = 0.811 \text{ ebits}$$

This is less than 1 ebit (the Bell state value), confirming the state is entangled but not maximally so. For comparison: separable states give $S_E = 0$, and Bell states give $S_E = 1$.

### Concurrence

**Definition 8.4.7 (Concurrence)**
For a two-qubit pure state $|\psi\rangle = \sum_{ij} c_{ij}|ij\rangle$, the **concurrence** is:

$$\mathcal{C}(|\psi\rangle) = |c_{00}c_{11} - c_{01}c_{10}|$$

For mixed states, the concurrence is defined via a more complex formula involving eigenvalues of $\rho(\sigma_y \otimes \sigma_y)\rho^*(\sigma_y \otimes \sigma_y)$.

**Properties:**
- $\mathcal{C} = 0$ for separable states.
- $\mathcal{C} = 1$ for maximally entangled states (Bell states).
- Related to entanglement of formation: $E_F = h\left(\frac{1 + \sqrt{1-\mathcal{C}^2}}{2}\right)$ where $h$ is the binary entropy.

**Example 8.4.12 (Concurrence of a Bell State)**
For $|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$, the coefficient matrix is:

$$C = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \det C = \frac{1}{2}$$

Using the standard formula $\mathcal{C} = 2|\det C|$ (see Exercise 8.4.10):

$$\mathcal{C} = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1$$

This confirms Bell states are maximally entangled ($\mathcal{C} = 1$).

**Example 8.4.13 (Concurrence of a Partially Entangled State)**
For $|\psi\rangle = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}|00\rangle + \frac{1}{2}|11\rangle$ (from Example 8.4.8), the coefficient matrix is:

$$C = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \det C = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}$$

$$\mathcal{C} = 2|\det C| = 2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.866$$

Since $0 < \mathcal{C} < 1$, the state is entangled but not maximally. For comparison: a separable state like $|00\rangle$ has $C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, giving $\mathcal{C} = 2 \cdot 0 = 0$.

### Summary of Entanglement Measures

| **Measure** | **Range** | **Separable** | **Max Entangled** | **Applicability** |
|-------------|-----------|---------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Schmidt Rank | $1, 2, \ldots, d$ | $r = 1$ | $r = d$ | Pure states |
| Entanglement Entropy | $[0, \log_2 d]$ | $S_E = 0$ | $S_E = \log_2 d$ | Pure bipartite |
| Concurrence | $[0, 1]$ | $\mathcal{C} = 0$ | $\mathcal{C} = 1$ | Two-qubit states |
| Negativity | $[0, \log_2 d]$ | $\mathcal{N} = 0$ | $\mathcal{N} > 0$ | Mixed states (general) |

> **Quantum Connection:** In quantum machine learning, entanglement entropy and expressivity are linked. Variational quantum circuits with higher entanglement can represent more complex functions, but excessive entanglement can make training difficult (barren plateau problem).

## Applications to Quantum Computing and ML

### Entanglement in Quantum Algorithms

1. **Quantum Speedup:** Many quantum algorithms (Shor's, Grover's) rely on entanglement to explore exponentially many states simultaneously.

2. **Quantum Error Correction:** Stabilizer codes (e.g., Shor code, surface code) use entangled ancilla qubits to detect and correct errors without measuring the logical qubit directly.

3. **Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE):** Entangling gates in the ansatz circuit enable the approximation of ground states of molecular Hamiltonians.

### Entanglement in Quantum Machine Learning

1. **Expressivity:** Quantum neural networks gain expressivity from entanglement. More entanglement → more complex decision boundaries.

2. **Barren Plateaus:** Excessive entanglement in random circuits leads to vanishing gradients, making training difficult.

3. **Quantum Kernel Methods:** Entanglement in feature maps creates high-dimensional Hilbert spaces, enabling powerful kernel methods.

```
Entanglement Tradeoff in QML:

Low Entanglement:
  + Easy to simulate classically
  + Gradients well-behaved
  - Limited expressivity

High Entanglement:
  + High expressivity
  + Quantum advantage possible
  - Barren plateaus
  - Hard to train

Optimal: Structured entanglement tailored to problem
```

## Summary

**Key Concepts:**
- **Entanglement** is a non-classical correlation between quantum systems that cannot be factored into independent subsystems.
- **Bell states** are maximally entangled two-qubit states forming an orthonormal basis.
- **EPR paradox** and **Bell's theorem** show that quantum mechanics cannot be explained by local hidden variables.
- **CHSH inequality** provides an experimental test: quantum correlations can violate classical bounds.
- **No-communication theorem** ensures entanglement cannot transmit information faster than light.
- **Quantum teleportation** and **superdense coding** use entanglement as a resource for communication.
- **Schmidt decomposition** quantifies entanglement via Schmidt coefficients and rank.
- **Entanglement entropy** and **concurrence** provide computable measures of entanglement.

**Connections to ML:**
- Entanglement enables quantum speedup and expressivity in quantum ML models.
- Understanding entanglement is crucial for designing efficient quantum circuits and avoiding training pitfalls.
